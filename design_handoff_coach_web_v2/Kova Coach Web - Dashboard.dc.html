Roles · what the launchpad becomes for everyone else

You're right that desktop mostly means programming, so the shape holds. The fix is that the four launch cards shouldn't be a fixed grid — they're generated from what the account can actually do, so nobody gets a card that leads to a permission wall. Three rules cover every combination you have: Pay is Approve for admins and Log my hours for everyone else, since every coach logs their own; Run the gym only exists for admins and its slot goes to whichever module that person actually runs; and Needs you plus the roster strip are scoped to their own clients, so a coach never gets nudged about someone else's. Resume-first doesn't change for anyone — only what you resume into does.

Role variants
2aCoach with SPC | programs, doesn't run the gym
The common case. Programming is untouched — same resume card, same grid and builder routes. Pay flipped from Approve to Log my hours and now carries what he owes rather than what he owns. The fourth slot, which was Staff & settings, went to SPC because that's the module he actually runs; a coach without SPC would get three cards, not a dead one. The old dashboard's client alerts got out of hand, so this list is deliberately small and mostly informational: his own timesheet pins to the top because it's the only line that's genuinely his to close, and below it sit at most three client notes — a fact and a clause, no buttons, no dismiss chrome, nothing that reads as an inbox to zero out. Everything else stays in Clients, where a list belongs.

1fPayroll close | review each person, then close once
You described the job as review, approve or send back, then close — so the screen is that sequence and nothing else. One row per staff member with the work already broken out by category — hours where the category is hours, session counts where it is sessions, and Other as a plain amount, because Other is not one thing — it is a list of named types you define in Payroll → Settings, each with its own rate. This is the one place that gets a real typed confirm, because it snapshots rates and can't be undone.

1oMerge exercises | its own page, and you can merge anything
The detector's guesses shouldn't be the only way in, so merging is a page rather than a banner. Dismissed pairs collect at the bottom, reversible, so "these are different" is a decision you can see rather than something that silently vanished.

1qNutrition client | the dashboard tab
Trends is gone as a tab — it was this, in a worse place. The chart group below is the trends view, so the tabs are Dashboard, Weeks, Check-In, Photos and Targets. Click any of the four small charts and it swaps into the large slot, with weight dropping down to take its place: one chart is always big, you choose which, and nothing scrolls away. This is what Dashboard should answer: is she logging, is she moving, and is anything owed. The rings are the same ones the member sees, so a number you discuss on a call looks identical on both screens. Weight gets the large chart because it's the number the programme turns on. The range chips (W, 1m, 3m, 6m, 1y) moved out of that card and up into a Trends bar, because they govern every chart below them, not just the big one — sleep, steps, hunger and energy are on the same window, and each carries the range as a small label so a screenshot of one card can't lie about what it covers. On 3m the small cards compare against that window's average rather than last week, which is why hunger reads "avg 2.4" against today's 4. The rings above stay on 7 days no matter what, since a macro average over three months isn't a number anyone acts on. Every bar is hoverable and gives its real reading — date and value, shown here on July 21 — so you never have to eyeball a height. The right rail is the coaching layer rather than a copy of the other tabs: Focus (the checklist she sees on her own screen, which the check-in snapshots), the game plan in your words, and milestones — three active at a time because the cap is what keeps them meaningful. Targets, check-in and photos aren't repeated here; each has a tab, and the header badge already says where the check-in stands. This is the read; Targets is where numbers change, Plan is where the long arc lives.

1rWeeks | every week, and the days inside one
The week is the unit a nutrition programme actually moves in, so this is the spine of the client record: one row per week with average weight and its change, how many days she logged, the macro averages against target as small bars, and where the check-in got to. Any row opens into its seven days — the same numbers the member sees on her own weekly screen, so a conversation about Thursday is about the same Thursday. Unlogged days stay visibly blank rather than being interpolated or counted as zero, and the averages say plainly that they only cover the days she filled in. Target changes are drawn as bands between the weeks they took effect, because "she stalled" and "you moved her carbs" are the same story and reading it needs both.

1uCheck-In | her week, against your plan
The queue on the Nutrition page is for moving through check-ins; this is the same check-in inside the client record, for when you arrived from her profile instead. Two cards sit above the metrics. On the left, any photos that came with this check-in, thumbnailed with the day’s weight and a route into the Photos tab for the real comparison — a check-in that arrived with pictures should never look identical to one that did not, and when there are none the card is not rendered — the targets card then takes the full width rather than sitting half-empty next to a gap. On the right, how her week landed against target, drawn as the same rings the member app uses and the Dashboard tab repeats, so the shape you glance at is identical wherever you meet it. Metrics run across the top with their change from last week. Written answers show verbatim with last week greyed underneath, hers-only questions marked, and the ones that did not change collapsed at the bottom — same rule as the queue card, no summarising. Her focus items are shown as they were when she submitted, frozen, so editing them later cannot rewrite what she was actually working to. The rail carries the game plan rather than a reply box: what you want in view while reading her week is what you already decided to do. Replying goes through the floating message bubble, not a second inbox that exists only on this tab, so completing the check-in is the single action here.

1vPhotos | sets by date, and the compare
Photos are only useful next to each other, so the comparison is the page — two large frames across the whole upper half rather than thumbnails you click into. The rail underneath is deliberately text: someone photographing herself weekly for a year has 150-odd images, and a thumbnail grid would mean a slow page on every single visit. Dated chips carry the weight from that day and cost nothing to render, so the only images ever fetched are the two you are actually looking at. Click a chip to load it into the left or right frame; the year switcher pages the rail instead of growing it. A set that is missing an angle says so on the row and offers the nudge, which is the actual fix — the old flow let a half-set sit there silently until you tried to compare and found nothing to compare against. Ticking two rows loads them side by side with the angle switcher on top, and the difference in weight and elapsed weeks is computed rather than left for you to work out.

1wTargets | the numbers, and why they moved
Changing targets is the one number-editing job in the client record, so it gets its own tab rather than living inside a check-in. Each macro shows the target with her seven-day average underneath, so you are editing next to the evidence instead of from memory. The screen shows the numbers and nothing else — no suggestions, no “carbs look low” prompts. Reading them is the job you do. The note is required-shaped rather than optional, because a target change without a reason is what makes the history unreadable six months later — but it is yours: it is not sent to her and there is no notification when targets change. She sees the new numbers in the app the next time she logs, the way she would any other week. Calories are the awkward one — raising carbs moves them unless you say otherwise, so there is an explicit recalculate rather than a silent arithmetic surprise. History is the real payoff: what moved, who moved it, and why, which is exactly the context the Weeks tab bands point back to.
